                  SURVIVOR JUSTICE TAX PREVENTION ACT

  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Nebraska.


                                  NDAA

  Mrs. FISCHER. Mr. President, America faces the most perilous threat 
environment since World War II. We face an axis of aggressors--China, 
Russia, North Korea, Iran. Competition with these adversaries will 
determine whether the 21st century will be led by America or by an 
authoritarian regime. That is why we must be ready to deter and win any 
conflict.
  Every year, the Senate Armed Services Committee works together to 
build a strong Defense policy bill--the NDAA--to meet the current 
threat environment. We have done so successfully for 66 years in a row. 
I am proud that we have consistently produced a bipartisan product.

[[Page S5787]]

  Given the stakes, you would think that every Senator in both parties 
would see the urgency this moment demands; you would think every 
Senator would want to move the NDAA swiftly. Well, back in July, we had 
the opportunity to do just that. We could have considered additional 
amendments from Senators that may have improved the bill. I certainly 
wanted to take up amendments and have an open process on the Senate 
floor. Instead, Senator Schumer blocked the NDAA from moving forward.
  This is not a partisan bill. In fact, the managers' package alone 
included amendments from Members on both sides of the aisle--38 from 
Republicans, 38 from Democrats--and 10 that were crafted on a 
bipartisan basis.
  It was disappointing to see Senator Schumer put politics above our 
national security. Democrats prevented their own Members from offering 
amendments on issues that those Members and their constituents care 
about.
  I chair the Strategic Forces Subcommittee, and our portion of this 
year's NDAA addressed 184 requests from Senators on both sides of the 
aisle. I believe a full floor process would have strengthened the 
Senate's bill. Nonetheless, I am optimistic that we will soon reach 
agreement with the House on a final NDAA for this fiscal year.
  Thankfully, we will soon have another opportunity to consider this 
year's NDAA. It is time that we put our country first. It is time to 
prepare to debate and advance a bill that our national security depends 
on.
  I emphasize this because for the first time in history, the United 
States faces two peer nuclear adversaries at once.
  Russia has already modernized most of its nuclear forces. It 
continues to field new delivery systems and has long maintained an 
advantage in tactical nuclear weapons. That imbalance presents real 
challenges for deterrence.
  China is expanding its nuclear arsenal at a breathtaking pace, 
consistently exceeding U.S. estimates of their progress. China is 
building new missile silos, it is increasing its warhead stockpile, and 
it is rapidly developing a full range of nuclear delivery systems.
  This isn't the nuclear strategic environment we planned for. The 
structure of our nuclear forces today was determined at a very 
different geopolitical moment. At that time, policymakers believed that 
Russia might become a cooperative partner and that China would remain 
in a more limited strategic competitor mode.
  Those assumptions have been proven wrong. Russia has made clear its 
disdain for partnership. Instead, it prioritizes challenging the West. 
China is now an increasingly capable strategic rival. President Xi is 
working with our persistent adversaries to share weapons, to share 
technology, to share resources and strategic objectives. And together, 
they are working to challenge American power, to undermine stability, 
and to threaten international security.
  That reality should inject urgency into everything we do. That 
involves force modernization, industrial capacity, alliance 
coordination, and modernizing the bedrock of our national security, 
which is our nuclear triad.
  It is more important now than ever that we invest in our deterrence, 
in our missile defense systems, and that we support our allies. This 
year's NDAA does exactly that. And I am hopeful that my provisions to 
maintain the credibility of our nuclear deterrent will be retained in 
that final version.
  Given these threats, the President should have a full array of 
options to respond to limited nuclear strikes and deter our adversaries 
from employing this ``escalate-to-deescalate'' strategy. That is why I 
have fought to authorize over $250 million for the nuclear-armed sea-
launched cruise missile. I was glad to see that this year's Defense 
Appropriations Act adequately funded this program at the level that it 
does require.
  You know authorizing these systems and capabilities in the NDAA--hey, 
that is only the first step. Then we must actually appropriate the 
funds so that we can get these programs up and running. It is why I 
authorized the deployment of two additional theater-range nuclear 
capabilities. Without them, the United States risks ceding strategic 
advantages to our adversaries in regional conflicts.
  A modern, credible nuclear deterrent means little, though, without 
the President's ability to command, control, and communicate with those 
forces. So we have to also modernize our NC3 systems as well.
  This bill would push the Air Force to deliver an acquisition strategy 
for an airborne command post--a capability we need to command and 
control our nuclear forces in a crisis.
  China and Russia, well, they are also expanding the warfighting 
domain that we have in space. Our adversaries are fielding an array of 
weapons that can blind, jam, disable, or destroy our satellites. And 
Russia--Russia--is developing satellites to carry nuclear weapons into 
space.
  This threat is serious. We must build resilient satellite 
constellations that can survive attack. We must improve our ability to 
detect and then to track hostile activity in orbit. We must develop 
defensive and offensive capabilities.
  Adversaries cannot believe that they will ever have an advantage in 
space. Well, all of this requires substantial investment. Homeland 
defense is another priority that is reflected in this bill.
  Our adversaries have systems that are capable of holding U.S.-based 
assets at risk. The more vulnerable that our homeland is, the harder it 
becomes to employ power during conflict, and that brings us to Golden 
Dome.
  The United States needs a stronger homeland missile defense system. 
In 2024, I introduced legislation requiring the development of a 
comprehensive integrated air and missile defense architecture.
  My bill was enacted into law through the NDAA 2 years ago by a vote 
of 85 to 14. So it was Congress that established this program, and we 
did that in 2024.
  When President Trump began his second term in 2025, he brought the 
attention to this very real need by establishing what he then named 
Golden Dome for America.
  His focus here is welcome because the threat is real, and the threat 
is growing. China has built the world's most advanced hypersonic 
arsenal, and China has historically run up to 20 times more test 
flights than we have. It should alarm Americans that our adversaries 
are racing ahead with these weapons.
  Today, we have a very limited number of ground-based interceptors in 
Alaska and in California, and they protect us against an ICBM attack 
from a rogue nation, but they were never built to stop advanced cruise 
missiles or hypersonics from our peer adversaries, who are China and 
Russia.
  So that is why it gives us another reason why we have to have Golden 
Dome. It is essential.
  As chair of the Strategic Forces Subcommittee, I am working closely 
with General Guetlein and the Department leadership as they finalize 
the design, the schedule, and the long-term funding needs. We provided 
over $24 billion last time, last year as a downpayment for Golden Dome.
  I used this year's NDAA to keep the program on track. We cannot 
afford delay. We are going to have to have sustained investment for 
years to come. Taken together, all of these provisions, they send a 
clear message: America's capabilities remain strong and credible, but 
again, passing the NDAA is only part of protecting our national 
security. And then, we must fund these programs to get the job done.
  You know, a CR, a continuing resolution, it doesn't just pause these 
needed security programs; a continuing resolution sets them back, and 
it puts us even further behind our adversaries. Our adversaries know 
that.
  We cannot simply just talk about national security; we have to put 
the money behind all of our big talk. We face serious challenges as we 
navigate our adversaries' growing ambitions on land, across the seas, 
in the skies, and into outer space.
  But with strong leadership, with sustained investment, we can protect 
our country. We can advance our prosperity. But we must strengthen our 
military, and we must signal that America is prepared to meet the 
moment.
  So I urge my colleagues on both sides of the aisle. Put aside this 
partisan gamesmanship. Let's get the NDAA passed before the end of the 
year and then pay for what we need to do.

[[Page S5788]]

  I yield the floor.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Florida.
