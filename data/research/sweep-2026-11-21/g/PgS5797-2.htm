                  SURVIVOR JUSTICE TAX PREVENTION ACT

  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Utah.


                               H.R. 2347

  Mr. LEE. Madam President, we are approaching a very significant 
moment, an opportunity to pass the permanent reform bill commonly known 
as BAAJA. BAAJA is all about giving the American people the freedom to 
build, the freedom to build things the American people need.

[[Page S5798]]

  Over the last five or six decades, we have had some laws passed with 
great intentions that do good things. Some of those laws, based on the 
way they have been interpreted, based on the way they have been 
employed in litigation, have sometimes led to needless, prolonged, 
extensive, and ultimately very expensive delays.
  Those delays deprive many Americans of access to things they need, 
whether it is electric power, access to a road, or something else. 
Those delays ultimately end up being costly, and they are factored into 
everything from the cost of electricity, to the cost of fuel, to the 
cost of everything else because everything else, whether it is mined, 
grown in a field, or manufactured--they all take energy inputs to 
produce. They all require access to roads. They all require access to 
things that themselves depend on a permitting process at the Federal 
level that comes to a natural end.
  So anytime we become a permission-based society where people have to 
go to the Federal Government seeking permission, seeking a ``Mother, 
may I?'' of sorts in order to do something, we have to be very careful 
that that permission requirement doesn't become an open-ended ticket to 
nowhere, an open-ended ticket to unlimited delay.
  These delays enrich really only one category of people: those who 
benefit from the complexity of the law, those who benefit from the 
delay. So if you are a lawyer engaged in certain types of litigation or 
a compliance specialist or a lobbyist involved in this very niche 
exercise of prolonging and delaying Federal permits required to build 
things, you benefit from these laws and you benefit from this system in 
which it can take years or sometimes decades just to get a permission 
slip from the Federal Government saying that a particular project may 
proceed. Everyone else suffers, including and especially America's poor 
and hard-working middle class, who find that everything they buy is 
more expensive.
  Now, there are a lot of reasons why things get more expensive. Some 
of it has to do with how much the Federal Government is spending. It 
doesn't help when we spend $2 trillion a year, every year, more than we 
take in. But some of it also has to do with the length of time it takes 
to achieve a Federal permit.
  Estimates are that this will save every American family, just on 
electric power cost alone, many hundreds of dollars a year once we can 
get BAAJA passed into law. But that is only the beginning of the story 
because this bill will affect a lot more than electric power. Literally 
everything you buy will be made potentially more affordable by this 
bill in a very meaningful, very material way.
  Now, this has not been an easy effort. This has been really a bill 
that is about 2 years in the making, many more years than that when you 
think of all the conceptual thought processes that went into it. But in 
earnest, this has been a process that has been bipartisan over the last 
year and a half.
  I have been privileged to work with my negotiating partners--with 
Senator Sheldon and with Senator Martin Heinrich, who is the ranking 
Democrat on the Energy and Natural Resources Committee, which I am 
currently honored to chair. I also worked closely with Senator Shelley 
Moore Capito, the chairman of the Environment and Public Works 
Committee, and her ranking Democrat on that committee, Sheldon 
Whitehouse, the distinguished Senator from the State of Rhode Island.
  This has not been an easy process, but it has been a rewarding 
process, and we and our hard-working staffs have put an enormous amount 
of effort into it.
  We have also benefited from input from Members of the House and the 
Senate. We will start with the Senators. Senators from all committees 
and all backgrounds of both political parties have had input on this, 
as have our counterparts in the House of Representatives.
  We have benefited also from technical assistance and guidance from 
White House personnel, the Department of the Interior, EPA, and the 
Department of Energy, just to name a few.
  It has been an honor and a pleasure to work with Senators Heinrich, 
Capito, Whitehouse, and so many others on this, and I am grateful to 
them and their hard-working staffs, who have burned the midnight oil 
for weeks, months, really the last few years to make this happen.
  Now, as far as the vote that will be taking place later tonight, I 
look forward to this happening. This is a big day for the American 
people. It may not be the case that every American wakes up every day 
and says: How many more minutes until BAAJA passes? Some are looking 
forward to that. This is the kind of bill that the typical hard-working 
American ought not have to worry about. Many have not yet heard of this 
bill. Whether you have heard of it or not, whether you are rooting for 
it or against it, you will benefit from this bill once it becomes law. 
And make no mistake, it is about to become law. Let's make it happen.
  Thank you.

  Mr. WHITEHOUSE. Mr. President, I rise today in support of the 
Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, of which I am one of 
the four co-authors.
  On November 3, Americans sent a clear message to their elected 
officials: Life has gotten too expensive, and they want relief. Energy 
costs, groceries, insurance premiums--I repeatedly heard about all of 
these things as I met with voters around the country over the past 
year. And I am pleased to say that BAAJA will directly address voters' 
concern over rising energy costs by bringing down electricity prices. 
It will also, over time, mitigate the effects that climate change-
driven extreme weather is having on property insurance premiums, 
grocery prices, healthcare costs, and more.
  There are a lot of myths and misinformation out there about BAAJA. 
Unfortunately, some of this misinformation has been delivered right 
here on the Senate floor. So I want to set the record straight about 
BAAJA--what it does and what it doesn't do.
  Let's start with the title that amends the National Environmental 
Policy Act, or NEPA. To hear BAAJA's critics, BAAJA would gut NEPA. It 
does no such thing.
  First, BAAJA would focus NEPA environmental reviews on the sorts of 
large greenfield projects that could actually have major effects on the 
environment. We exempt from NEPA small administrative projects, 
projects where government involvement is minimal, and projects that 
retrofit or replace existing infrastructure. For example, in Rhode 
Island, the NEPA environmental review process was estimated to add 
thousands of dollars to the cost of each replacement bus shelter. That 
is ridiculous, and it is part of the reason why the American Public 
Transportation Association supports BAAJA.
  In a similar streamlining vein, BAAJA would impose a unified review 
structure on Federal permitting under NEPA. BAAJA builds on existing 
timelines in NEPA and requires that all other Federal authorizations or 
permits be processed simultaneously and that decisions on these 
authorizations be rendered shortly following the NEPA environmental 
review process. It also imposes discipline on Agencies, with escalating 
penalties on Agencies that delay the process. Rather than wait 5 or 10 
years to get a bridge or tunnel permitted, BAAJA will reduce those 
timelines to a little over 2 years. Research by Columbia University, as 
well as analysis by the nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office, shows 
that BAAJA will reduce timelines for project approvals. And all 
Americans will benefit from that. Indeed, Columbia's research shows 
that transmission and clean energy projects will see their timelines 
accelerated the most, and this will benefit Americans in the form of 
cheaper electricity prices and less of the air pollution that harms our 
health and drives extreme weather and climate change.
  The environment, too, will benefit. This past winter, a major sewage 
line ruptured under the Potomac River, resulting in the largest sewage 
spill in American history. Problems with the line had been signaled by 
the State of Maryland over 7 years ago, but replacement had been 
delayed by an environmental review process that dragged on across three 
administrations and was ongoing when the rupture occurred. This is 
unacceptable.
  BAAJA does not, contrary to what its critics assert, gut public 
participation. BAAJA introduces a requirement that project sponsors 
submit a statement of stakeholder engagement as part of any complete 
application for an environmental review under NEPA. This will 
incentivize early stakeholder engagement and result in projects that 
are more respectful of community needs and interests. Similarly, BAAJA 
requires a public comment period for the sorts of large projects that 
go through the environmental impact statement process This is new and 
not currently required by law.
  BAAJA also does not eliminate redressability. What it does is cabin 
the ability to stop a project's construction to instances where some 
other substantive law--the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act, 
the Civil Rights Act are but three examples--or the Administrative 
Procedure Act is violated and where real harm is likely to occur. This 
makes sense, as NEPA is a purely procedural statute, and stopping a 
project just to force an Agency to fix paperwork errors makes no sense.
  Let's move on to the title that amends the Clean Water Act. Here 
again, BAAJA's critics argue that it guts section 401 of the Clean 
Water Act. This is not true. BAAJA preserves section 401 (a) through 
(d) exactly as written. It adds a new subsection (g), which creates a 
special regime for interstate gas pipelines and interstate transmission 
lines where the 401 certification process would be triggered by direct 
discharges from a point source and a State's conditioning authority 
would be limited to such direct discharges. This makes sense. Gas 
pipelines and transmission lines present a lower risk profile for 
serious effects on water quality compared to mines, oil infrastructure, 
and dams, to name but a few other types of projects. And gas pipelines 
and transmission lines have both fallen victim to State politics on 
occasion. The changes in this subsection are designed to focus States 
on the Clean Water Act essential: ensuring water quality.
  The next title of BAAJA amends the Endangered Species Act. It does 
two things. It codifies a definition of best available science very 
similar to the definition already used by the courts, and it creates a 
program for States to assume consultations under section 7. The same 
critics of BAAJA who defend State authority under section 401 of the 
Clean Water Act oppose this State assumption program. It has to be 
said:

[[Page S5802]]

You can't both support and oppose federalism. Many of our leading 
environmental statues, including the Clean Air Act and Clean Water Act, 
provide substantial authority to the States to implement them. 
Importing a bit of cooperative federalism into the Endangered Species 
Act is not inconsistent with our canon of environmental law.
  Indeed, States already assume NEPA reviews of Federal highway 
projects under a different State assumption program, and just like that 
program, the State assumption program in BAAJA requires that State 
authority be terminated if the State does not adequately implement the 
program.
  Following the ESA title of BAAJA, there is a title on permit 
certainty. Unlike the other titles, this is new law, and so I will 
spend a bit of extra time discussing it.
  Section 1401, and the rest of title IV's ``Project Certainty'' 
provisions, are intended to send a clear signal to Federal Agencies 
that Federal authorizations and permits are not political weapons. No 
administration, whether Democratic or Republican, should be making 
decisions about Federal permits for any project or class of projects 
based simply on their personal opinions about what kinds of projects 
they like politically and what projects they dislike politically. The 
job of Federal Agencies is to permit and regulate according to the 
procedures and standards in law, and project sponsors are entitled to 
expect that Agencies will not do so arbitrarily Sections 1401, 1402, 
and 1403 give that instruction to Agencies and provide substantial 
remedies intended to compensate and protect project sponsors if 
Agencies fail to act accordingly.
  Section 1401 establishes a default principle that Agencies should not 
lightly interfere with existing Federal authorizations or permits for 
projects that already have all final approvals and then lays out the 
circumstances in which an Agency may revoke, suspend, or change 
approvals for these projects. Those enumerated reasons include 
subsections (A) when taken in response to a court order; (B) when the 
permit holder has breached the terms of the permit or materially 
violated applicable law; (C) when the permit was obtained through fraud 
or material misrepresentations; (D) when ``necessary to prevent 
immediate, specific, urgent, substantial, and proximate harm or damage 
to life, property, human health, the environment, national security, or 
defense that is based on new information,'' (E) when requested by the 
permit holder; and (F) when ``taken pursuant to any applicable law or 
regulation.''
  I would like to particularly highlight the exception in subsection 
(F). This exception is intentionally broad because section 1401 is not 
meant to interfere with the work of Agencies acting in good faith to 
give effect to the statutes, regulations, guidance, and other authority 
within their jurisdiction. There are dozens if not hundreds of statutes 
that authorize Agencies to act in ways that will impact existing 
permits or affect the construction or operation of a fully permitted 
project, and Agencies retain all those discretionary and 
nondiscretionary authorities. Agencies can still apply and enforce 
statutes and regulations, including new ones that come into effect 
after this law is enacted and after a permit is in effect.
  If the EPA lawfully issues a new regulation that changes pollution 
standards under the Clean Air Act, it still can apply that regulation 
against the holders of existing permits. Similarly, nothing in this 
section interferes with OSHA's or PHMSA's ability to take any action 
authorized by any law or regulation. If an Agency discovers that a 
project is in violation of a law or that an authorization or 
determination was improperly granted in the first place, it can take 
lawful action against the project, even if doing so will have 
implications for the project's ability to operate at normal capacity. 
Nothing in section 1401 interferes with Agencies' ability to do things 
they are authorized to do by Federal laws like the Clean Air Act, Clean 
Water Act, Safe Drinking Water Act, and Endangered Species Act or by 
lawful regulations issued under those laws. Section 1401 and its 
remedies come into play only when an Agency tries to interfere with a 
project's authorizations, construction, or operation in an arbitrary 
and capricious fashion that is not authorized by any applicable law or 
regulation.

  The exception in subsection (D) further clarifies the line between 
reasonable and arbitrary agency interference with issued permits. 
Agencies can--and should--act swiftly to prevent danger to life, human 
health, the environment, property, or national security or defense, but 
they must identify risks that are specific, near-term, and 
substantial--not vague, remote, or hypothetical. An Agency can 
intervene to prevent an imminent risk of human exposure to or 
environmental release of chemicals like PFAS pollutants, which are 
known to cause cancer and harm to childhood development; it cannot 
revoke permits based on general, cryptic assertions that exposures or 
risks may someday arise.
  In sum, the goal of section 1401 is to ensure that Agencies cannot 
use Federal permits to pick winners and losers in a manner divorced 
from law--and, importantly, to provide projects sponsors with 
significant remedies if Agencies try to do so. It will not interfere 
with the important regulatory and enforcement work that Agencies do 
under existing statutes.
  Section 1401 also provides additional protection to five offshore 
wind projects that have already been the subject of Federal 
interference and have had that interference enjoined by a court order. 
This added protection is outlined in the ``Special Rule'' subsection 
and requires the Federal Government to obtain a court order before it 
takes any further action against any of these projects.
  Offshore wind projects are not the only projects that have found 
themselves caught up in an overly politicized permitting process. Wind 
projects have been stalled at DOD, and wind and solar projects have 
been stalled at Interior. I appreciate the White House's cooperation in 
reaching a political agreement during our permitting reform 
negotiations to advance land-based wind projects that had been stalled 
at DOD for more than a year. The mitigation agreements DOD has now 
executed and the other wind projects it has cleared were all 
specifically part of that political agreement. While I welcome these 
actions, they reflect the commitments made by the White House, rather 
than a broader resumption of DOD's normal review process. We have not 
seen comparable progress outside the agreement.
  Importantly, DOD is executing mitigation agreements negotiated before 
the freeze began, without imposing additional national security 
mitigation requirements That raises serious questions about why these 
projects were stalled in the first place, particularly given DOD's 
shifting national security explanations for the delays. Approximately 
300 other projects remain stalled. DOD must resume its normal review 
process for all projects, not just those covered by the political 
agreement. Developers should not need a political agreement to receive 
the reviews and decisions required by law.
  If DOD does not resume processing mitigation agreements for wind 
projects and Interior does not resume the permitting of renewable 
projects, they will run afoul of section 1402 of BAAJA, which imposes 
large penalties on the Federal Government when it engages in a pattern 
of disparate treatment in the permitting process, either through 
delaying decisions beyond the applicable deadline or denying 
authorizations. Such delays or denials have to have substantially 
increased across the specific class of project, as has been the case 
for wind and solar projects over the last 2 years. Section 1402 allows 
for a 6-month grace period before a cause of action may be brought 
under it for delayed or denied non-NEPA authorizations and a 9-month 
grace period for NEPA authorizations, in order to allow time for 
Federal Agencies to get back to permitting these projects. The 
administration has assured us that it understands that it must process 
applications or face massive financial penalties under section 1402. We 
will be watching vigilantly to make sure that they keep their word or 
face the consequences this section imposes.
  Finally, section 1403 creates a new 1-year unified timeline for all 
non-NEPA Federal authorizations. This is important, as there are a 
plethora of Federal authorizations, and any one of them could be 
weaponized by an administration hostile to a particular type of

[[Page S5803]]

project. Creating a timeline offers them protection under section 1402 
and has the further benefit of injecting discipline into often slow, 
unaccountable Agencies and inter-agency process.
  I will not spend as much time discussing the titles of this bill in 
the Energy Committee's jurisdiction, as they do not relate to the 
committee on which I am the ranking member. Suffice it to say, they are 
excellent. They will simplify and streamline the siting and permitting 
of long-distance transmission lines, help unclog the interconnection 
queue, and force data centers to pay for the costs they impose on the 
grid. Taken together, these provisions are modeled to save ratepayers 
more than $10 billion per year--an enormous down payment on 
affordability--and will reduce power sector emissions on a gigaton 
scale, up to 20 percent.
  BAAJA marks a major step in facilitating our Nation's clean energy 
renaissance, a renaissance that will lower costs, create well-paying 
jobs, reduce pollution, and fight climate change. It will also create 
rigor in an otherwise too-often politicized and unaccountable 
permitting process, resulting in faster timelines for infrastructure, 
housing, and so much more. Let's pass this bill.
  Ms. SMITH. Mr. President, I rise today to express my deep concern 
that the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 poses 
significant threats to Tribal sovereignty as well as to one of our 
Nation's most precious wild places in my home State of Minnesota.
  On its face, this bill is aimed at increasing domestic energy 
production, making our electric grid more resilient, and lowering costs 
for American families--all goals I share. However, the devil is in the 
details, and I believe this bill does a lot of harm.
  I have always said, and I still believe, that we need permitting 
reform. We need to update our outdated permitting process so we can 
lower energy costs by getting cleaner, cheaper power connected to the 
grid, but this bill is a bad deal that Democrats should not take. 
Language in this bill would not only make it much easier for 
Antofagasta to build its copper-sulfide mine in the watershed of our 
precious Boundary Waters, but also put at risk sacred historical sites 
of Tribal Nations and violate their sovereignty. We can reform 
permitting without violating Tribal sovereignty or sacrificing our 
water quality and precious public lands, in Minnesota and around the 
country.
  A cornerstone responsibility of the Federal Government is to respect 
the sovereign rights of Tribal Nations and to adhere to our legal 
obligations defined in our treaties and trust agreements with Native 
Nations. This bill, unfortunately, was negotiated without consultation 
with Tribes, and as a result, it undermines our responsibilities for 
consultation and cooperation with Tribes. I have no doubt that all of 
you have heard about this directly from Tribal leaders over the last 
several weeks since the first text of this bill came out. I urge you to 
hear these concerns and remember that honoring Tribal sovereignty must 
be a constant in our work, not an option.
  For Tribal Nations in Minnesota and across the country, this bill is 
an imminent threat to their cultural resources. For decades, the 
National Historic Preservation Act has protected Tribal cultural 
resources and other historic properties through a collaborative process 
with Tribes, States, and Federal Agencies. This process protects our 
Nation's historic resources, and in most cases, it moves quickly and 
efficiently. Tribal historic preservation officers estimate that the 
average time to complete their historic review process is 16 days, and 
98 percent of projects are reviewed on time.
  Now, I am not opposed to addressing impediments in this process where 
there are legitimate problems. But the changes made in this bill are 
unnecessarily broad and sweeping. They strike at the heart of the law 
by taking away Tribes' and States' power in the historic preservation 
review process. And once damage is done to historic sites, it can be 
impossible to undo. That is what we risk by passing this bill.
  I am also gravely concerned that this bill will result in 
irreversible pollution from mining near the Boundary Waters Canoe 
Wilderness Area, or BWCA.
  The BWCA is an incredible wilderness, home to some of the cleanest 
water you will find in the lower 48 States. It is a cherished place to 
families in Minnesota and beyond, including mine, and it is within the 
ceded territory of the Grand Portage, Bois Forte, and Fond du Lac Bands 
of Lake Superior Chippewa. I know many of my colleagues have spent time 
in the BWCA, either as children or adults.
  This place is too special to risk greenlighting a foreign-owned 
company with a sketchy track record because, under the terms of this 
legislation, the Trump administration could greenlight illegal leases 
and permits to mine over the next 2 years, leaving virtually no 
recourse to stop the Twin Metals mine, which a foreign mining 
conglomerate hopes to build on the doorstep of the Boundary Waters.
  I am not anti-mining; Minnesota is a mining State. But we cannot 
tolerate this mine in this place. The science and data have confirmed 
over many years that a copper sulfide mine on the boundaries of the 
Boundary Waters Canoe Wilderness Area poses an unacceptable 
environmental risk to the area.
  The Trump administration and Republicans in this Congress have 
already revoked protections for this special place. This bill, should 
it become law, would loosen existing legal protections and create a 
pathway for the Trump administration to fast-track the approval and 
construction of this mine. Furthermore, language in this bill would 
make it nearly impossible for future administrations to take necessary 
actions to regulate or rescind approvals for the mine--even if they are 
made unlawfully by Trump administration--something we can and should 
fully expect.
  For the Grand Portage, Bois Forte, and Fond du Lac Tribal Nations, 
this mine also poses threats to their treaty-protected rights to hunt, 
fish, and gather in their ceded territory, which covers the BWCA and 
surrounding areas.
  Central to the Ojibwe tradition is wild rice, or manoomin, a sacred 
food found on fresh water in the Great Lakes Region. Manoomin is highly 
susceptible to sulfate pollution, the exact type of pollution we risk 
from this proposed mine. This bill undermines the Tribal Nations' 
ability to protect their treaty resources from mining, and that is 
unacceptable.
  We need permitting reform, but I cannot support this bill in its 
current form, and I urge my colleagues supporting this bill to 
reconsider your position and vote no. Let us continue to negotiate and 
work for a commonsense bill that reforms permitting so we can build the 
clean energy projects we so desperately need, while also protecting the 
sovereign rights of Tribes and the precious places we all love so much.
