         BIPARTISAN AMERICAN AFFORDABILITY AND JOBS ACT OF 2026

  Mr. HEINRICH. Mr. President, thank you for the opportunity to submit 
this statement for the record regarding the Bipartisan American 
Affordability and Jobs Act.
  I want to speak a little about changes we made to this legislation 
after hearing concerns from the public.
  On section 1401, the permitting certainty provision, some folks were 
concerned that it would inoculate fast-

[[Page S5808]]

tracked, problematic projects from being undone or fixed by a future 
administration.
  Essentially, they feared that this administration could abuse the 
legislative language to greenlight bad projects early and make it 
impossible to fix them.
  To prevent that, we amended BAAJA to ensure that only permitted and 
approved projects with all of their Federal permits--not just some--are 
protected by the certainty provision.
  This makes it crystal clear that having a lease alone does not 
qualify a project for section 1401 certainty. The project now must also 
have all of their Federal permits approved to qualify for the certainty 
provided under section 1401.
  This means that for a place like the Boundary Waters, where the Trump 
administration has been trying to allow mining, or for the Arctic 
National Refuge, where pristine habitat is at risk of being developed, 
companies will have to have wait for all of their Federal permits to be 
fully approved and legal before they can take advantage of the 
permitting bill's certainty protections.
  A second concern was that updated, strengthened environmental 
regulations in the future wouldn't be reflected in existing permits.
  So we amended the permitting legislation to make it clear that 
Federal Agencies can update permits when environmental rules change.
  This would mean that the Environmental Protection Agency, for 
example, could require powerplants to meet updated clean air standards 
or for mines to meet updated mining standards.
  Third, we heard from Tribes across the country that they felt as if 
section 106 was stripped of its teeth.
  I have heard directly from Tribal leaders in my State about the 
importance of having a meaningful voice in decisions that affect their 
communities and their lands.
  So we worked to dramatically strengthen that in our amendments.
  Tribes will now have an equal seat with States in transmission 
planning.
  And Federal Agencies will be required to consult early and 
meaningfully with Tribes on projects that will affect important 
cultural sites, including on what properties are affected, the impacts 
of the project, and ways to avoid, minimize, or mitigate potential 
harm.
  That means mandatory Tribal consultation is built into the section 
106 process at every stage, by law.
  It isn't a ``may''--it is a ``shall.''
  And it is a rejection of this administration's proposed section 106 
regulatory reforms--specifically rejecting their regulatory proposal to 
turn Tribal consultation into a public comment period.
  The language in this legislation will force the administration to go 
through the Tribal consultation process early and often. And it will 
ensure that the consultation process involves meaningful engagement--
not rushed, performative exercises to simply ``check the box.''
  That is also why we extended the window for objections to Agency 
reports from 30 days to 45 days and doubled the authorized funding for 
the Historic Preservation Fund--because we want to ensure that the 
consultation remains robust.
  This legislation took many months to negotiate. And I am proud of the 
work we did both before we introduced it and after.
  I appreciate my colleague's working with me to achieve these 
important improvements in the legislation.
