What S. 5653 would change, the bill sections behind each provision, and how supporters and critics read them.

What would the bill change?

Review deadlines

Sets 2-year EIS and 1-year assessment deadlines, followed by deadlines for agency decisions. Bill p. 46, line 15 to p. 46, line 18

Extensions and enforcement conditions apply; the travel sanction limits official trips beyond 25 miles after a qualifying deadline is 60 days overdue. Bill p. 54, line 17 to p. 54, line 20

Lawsuit limits

Generally allows 150 days for NEPA challenges in a court of appeals, with direct-harm and conditional comment requirements. Bill p. 67, line 24 to p. 68, line 2

Limits remedies to remand, with a fix due within 180 days; preliminary injunctions remain available under specified conditions involving other law. Bill p. 69, line 24 to p. 70, line 1

Permit certainty

Restricts interference with permits in effect on or after September 16, 2026, subject to listed exceptions including the holder’s request. Bill p. 159, line 12 to p. 159, line 15

If the agency fails to meet its burden in court, the sponsor is entitled to specified relief, including a court-determined 25 to 50 percent of incurred project costs not otherwise recovered. Bill p. 164, line 3 to p. 164, line 6

Data center costs

Newly connected data-center loads of 20 megawatts or more must cover their incremental electricity costs. Bill p. 235, line 12 to p. 235, line 13

Previously approved service arrangements remain in effect. Bill p. 240, line 5 to p. 240, line 8

The bill recognizes state authority to require new generation. Bill p. 237, line 14 to p. 237, line 17

Transmission siting

FERC can permit interstate lines of 230 kilovolts and up that states have not approved, after making required findings, and planning regions must plan lines with their neighbors. Bill p. 189, line 17 to p. 189, line 19

Actions outside NEPA

Excludes specified loan-only actions, qualifying work in existing grid and pipeline corridors, and listed oil, gas and geothermal activities from NEPA. Bill p. 204, line 17 to p. 204, line 21

Each exclusion has its own scope and conditions. Bill p. 204, line 19 to p. 204, line 21

Clean Water Act

Narrows state water-quality certification review and generally limits it to one year; extensions require written agreement. Bill p. 114, line 20 to p. 114, line 22

Extends nationwide permits to 10 years. Bill p. 125, line 2 to p. 125, line 3

Endangered Species Act

Cuts the standard formal consultation period from 90 days to 60, with extension rules. Bill p. 134, line 16 to p. 134, line 17

Lets approved state programs assume specified consultation responsibilities; applicants can opt for federal consultation. Bill p. 148, line 7 to p. 148, line 10

Historic preservation

Narrows the effects covered by historic-preservation review and sets deadlines. Bill p. 380, line 13 to p. 380, line 16

Adds a 150-day challenge window and limits remedies, with separate rules for missed deadlines. Bill p. 408, line 23 to p. 408, line 25

Digital permitting

Requires a public tracker of defined transparency projects, and targets December 1, 2028 for an interagency ePermit system, to the maximum extent practicable. Bill p. 98, line 1 to p. 98, line 3

Data centers

How the bill impacts data centers, AI computing facilities and interconnection speed. Covers study automation, transmission rate carrots and sticks, grid-enhancing technologies, behind-the-meter resources, and ratepayer protections. Each row gives current policy, the change and the sections that make it.

Interconnection study automation and AI evaluation

Directs FERC to initiate a rulemaking within 1 year requiring public utility transmission providers to share and employ queue management practices using artificial intelligence, machine learning, and automation to evaluate and process interconnection requests faster, revisited every 4 years. Sec. 2110

15-day default deadline for grid study data

Adds Federal Power Act section 232. Bulk-power system users, owners, and operators must provide requested grid data within 15 days (unless FERC sets an alternative timeline for a data category) in a format the requester can use or convert. Confidentiality rules cannot be used to withhold data; violations by public utilities are subject to FPA section 316A reliability enforcement penalties, with other entities subject to FERC compliance orders. Sec. 2111

Standardized reference spec for fully automated studies

Requires FERC and the Department of Energy to publish, within 1 year, a joint reference specification for fully automated interconnection studies, including system impact studies, without supplemental data requests during the study process. Sec. 2111

Standardized cost-reduction options in upgrade studies

Requires FERC rules for a standardized analysis to identify upgrades for computational load sites and cost-reduction options. Sec. 2107

Mandatory transmission rate discount for non-firm computational load

Directs FERC to revise its 1994 pricing policy statement within 270 days to provide a lower cost of transmission service for computational loads that elect non-firm transmission service, followed by a proposed rule within 270 days and a final rule within 1 year. Sec. 2107

Standard firm transmission pricing baseline ('and' pricing)

Declares it unjust and unreasonable under FPA sections 205 and 206 to charge computational loads less than embedded costs plus incremental expansion costs ('and' pricing). Directs that incremental revenues be credited to the utility's annual transmission revenue requirement to prevent double recovery from other customers. Sec. 2107

Codification of flexible and interim transmission service

Statutorily defines transmission service categories under FPA section 228, explicitly including network integration, point-to-point, and any interim, non-firm, or contract demand service that limits the quantity of energy withdrawn or operating conditions. Sec. 2107

Mandatory deployment of advanced transmission technologies

Requires public utilities to use advanced transmission technologies in covered projects whenever benefits exceed costs, backed by a presumption of prudence and return-on-equity reduction penalties for non-compliance. Sec. 2109

NEPA and historic preservation categorical exclusion for reconductoring

Adds Federal Power Act section 216A: work that maintains or increases grid capacity in an existing right-of-way or necessary adjacent area (reconductoring, higher voltage, advanced conductors, added circuits) is designated as categorically excluded from NEPA and exempt from NHPA section 106 undertakings. Sec. 2102

Statutory wholesale market access for behind-the-meter power

Effective 18 months after enactment, declares it unjust and unreasonable under FPA sections 205 and 206 for any grid operator to prevent participation of qualifying behind-the-meter aggregations (100 kW minimum aggregation, 5 kW per unit, 15-minute response) in wholesale energy, capacity, or ancillary service markets. Sec. 2108

Device-level metering and inverter telemetry

Rules cannot require equipment owned or installed by the distribution utility; wholesale market participation may use device-level equipment and smart inverters for metering and telemetry. Sec. 2108

No allocation of incremental cost to other customers

Adds Federal Power Act section 228. For data centers and high-density computing loads of 20 MW or more that interconnect on or after enactment, no portion of the incremental generation, storage, transmission, or distribution cost may be allocated to or recovered in rates of any other customer. Existing approved arrangements remain grandfathered. Sec. 2107

Exit liability and up-front financial assurance

For loads connecting on or after enactment, utilities must collect financial assurance or upfront contributions before constructing facilities or upgrades. Cost recovery continues even if the data center terminates its contract or ceases electric purchases; existing arrangements are grandfathered. Sec. 2107

State authority to auction grid access and require new generation

For loads connecting on or after enactment, States may hold competitive solicitations or open seasons to allocate retail service among covered loads, charge more than incremental cost to rebate other customers, require bring-your-own new generation, and prioritize non-firm loads. Pre-enactment approved arrangements remain in effect. Sec. 2107

Mandatory EIA reporting on data center electricity load

Directs the Energy Information Administration to revise Form 861 to include electricity load from data center entities (AI, cloud, cryptocurrency) no later than 90 days before the first April 1 after enactment. Sec. 2114

Project delays

Studies of why energy projects take years, grouped by cause. Each row shows what the research found, the bill text aimed at that cause, and named analysts' readings of what the text would change. Where studies disagree, the row says so.

Environmental impact statements take years

CEQ reported a median of 2.2 years from notice of intent to final EIS for EISs finished in 2024; the 2017-2020 median was 3.1 years and 2021-2024 was 2.4 years. Council on Environmental Quality

CGO found clean-energy EISs averaged about three years from notice of intent to record of decision, with solar fastest (just under two years to final EIS) and hydroelectric slowest (5.1 years). Center for Growth and Opportunity (Utah State University)

RFF found solar and wind EISs averaged 26 and 43 months against 54 months across all project types, and most wind, solar and geothermal projects took over five years to become operational. Resources for the Future

CGEP, citing CEQ, put the 2019-2024 median EIS at 2.8 years, and noted agencies reported two EISs and 153 assessments missing the 2023 statutory deadlines between June 2023 and June 2025. Columbia University Center on Global Energy Policy

Lawsuits under NEPA

Ruple and Race found about one in 450 NEPA decisions (all decisions, including categorical exclusions) was litigated, and the challenge rate fell over the study period. University of Utah Wallace Stegner Center

Bennon and Wilson found 28% of projects requiring an EIS saw pre-development litigation, with the highest rates for solar (nearly two-thirds), pipelines (50%), wind (38%) and transmission (31%). Stanford University

RFF found nearly a third of solar and half of wind projects that completed EISs were sued, and agencies mostly won. Resources for the Future

Breakthrough found NEPA appeals averaged 4.2 years from the EIS or EA to the end of the appeal, with agencies winning about 80%. Breakthrough Institute

Interconnection queues

LBNL found the median time from interconnection request to commercial operation doubled from under 2 years (built 2000-2007) to over 4 years (built 2018-2024). Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory

LBNL found 13% of capacity requested 2000-2019 reached operation, 77% was withdrawn and 10% was still active. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory

Reporting LBNL's July 2026 update, APPA says request-to-agreement took well over 3 years in 2025 and request-to-operation exceeded 5 years in regions with data. LBNL's own page was not fetchable. American Public Power Association (reporting LBNL's next Queued Up edition)

Hausfather finds data centers that skip the grid for on-site gas generators have 60 to 80% higher emissions. He gives no figure for queue times. The Climate Brink

High-voltage transmission is built slowly

Grid Strategies found only 322 miles of 345 kV+ lines were completed in 2024, versus nearly 4,000 in 2013. Grid Strategies and Americans for a Clean Energy Grid

Gas pipelines averaged 534 miles a year over five years against 345 miles for high-voltage lines; the report links the gap to FERC's siting authority over pipelines. Grid Strategies and Americans for a Clean Energy Grid

The report cites DOE's study as calling for doubling regional and quadrupling interregional capacity by 2050, about 5,000 miles a year of regional lines. Grid Strategies and Americans for a Clean Energy Grid

Local and state opposition

Sabin counted at least 459 counties and municipalities in 44 states with severe local restrictions on renewables, up 16% in a year. Columbia University Sabin Center for Climate Change Law

The sixth Sabin report counts 567 contested projects since 2004, with about 40% cancelled; it says cancellations cannot all be attributed to local opposition. Columbia University Sabin Center (reported by pv magazine USA)

LBNL's developer survey ranked community opposition and local ordinances alongside grid interconnection as leading causes of delay and cancellation. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (reported by Latitude Media)

Third Way's survey found organized resistance affects a minority of projects, federal reviews caused the longest delays, and state and local permitting also slowed projects. Third Way (survey by ERM)

Adelman and co-authors conclude the empirical record shows environmental review and permitting rarely delay or block utility-scale renewables, citing the LBNL survey result that environmental regulations caused under 15% of delays or cancellations. Environmental Law Reporter

A Crux survey of 50 developers says federal permitting contributed to delays or cancellations in 94% of surveyed projects. Crux

Federal reversals of approved projects

The largest 2025 cancellation in Sabin's data, 6.2 GW Esmeralda 7 in Nevada, was cancelled by BLM. pv magazine USA (reporting Sabin Center)

AP reports the administration suspended five offshore wind projects in December and they later moved forward after federal judges intervened. Associated Press via PBS NewsHour

Historic preservation review (Section 106)

ACHP says over 120,000 undertakings go through Section 106 each year and over 95% resolve without ACHP involvement. Advisory Council on Historic Preservation

IFP says few undertakings are found to adversely affect historic properties, but those that do are disproportionately large energy and infrastructure projects. Institute for Progress

Adelman and co-authors note that there are almost no data or studies on how long Section 106 consultation takes. Environmental Law Reporter

Endangered species consultations

Li reported median consultation times of 13 days (informal) and 62 days (formal) from when FWS had enough information to start. Defenders of Wildlife

Li reported 1,381 formal consultations, 20%, exceeded the 135-day limit, many with agreed extensions. Defenders of Wildlife

Clean Water Act permits and state certification

EPA's inspector general found more than a third of the Corps individual permits in its sample took a year or longer. The sample is from 2011 and covers coal mining only. EPA Office of Inspector General

Agency staffing

A former Permitting Council director warned staff losses and expected funding cuts could undermine the federal permitting modernization plan. No headcount is given. Nextgov/FCW (quoting Eric Beightel, former Federal Permitting Council director)

IFP reports Utah's digital SHPO finishes about 98% of reviews within seven days while offices without modern systems lag badly. Institute for Progress

Adelman and co-authors argue that agency funding, staffing and training speed permitting for every kind of project. Environmental Law Reporter

How clean energy fares in federal review

RFF found solar, wind and geothermal reviews were historically shorter than other infrastructure, though other steps may take as long. Resources for the Future

Communities

How the bill changes who can comment, be consulted, take part in planning or go to court. Each row gives current law, the change and the sections that make it.

Comment period on a notice of intent for an EIS

Each notice of intent would be published in the Federal Register with a request for comment. The lead agency would set a comment period of 45 to 60 days after publication, or up to 75 days if the lead agency and the project sponsor agree. Sec. 1106

Combining categorical exclusions to skip a review

A lead agency could rely on one exclusion or a combination of several. Repeated use of the same or similar exclusions based on size, acreage or dollar thresholds would not count as a combination. An exclusion established by Congress would also qualify. The text adds no notice or comment step for relying on an exclusion. Sec. 1105

Actions defined as not a major Federal action

The list would grow to fourteen. New entries include projects whose only federal involvement is a loan or loan guarantee, and routine agency actions costing up to $10,000,000. Others cover lifeline infrastructure repair that adds no more than 20 percent capacity, geothermal observation tests, transmission activities described in a new section 216A(b) of the Federal Power Act and covered activities under Natural Gas Act section 7(i)(1). Actions another agency already evaluated under NEPA, and some Corps projects with a federal share of up to $50,000,000, also qualify. These actions fall outside the Act's review requirements. Sec. 1102

State or tribal review standing in for NEPA

An agency would not need an environmental document if the action was reviewed under a state or tribal environmental review law or policy and the lead agency finds the review serves a similar function. The same applies where another federal law serves a similar function. On a sponsor's request the lead agency must also consider, and as appropriate adopt, state or tribal analysis. The text does not say what notice or comment the earlier review must have had. Sec. 1105 · Sec. 1106

New science that arrives after a notice of intent

No federal agency would have to consider research that becomes public after the notice of intent or the decision to prepare an environmental document, and an agency could not delay past a deadline to wait for it. A savings clause leaves in place Administrative Procedure Act review of information in comments received during a comment period. Sec. 1106

Alternatives the public can ask an agency to study

The lead agency must eliminate any alternative, except no-action, that does not fulfill the purpose and need or meet the goals of an applicable non-federal sponsor. Cooperating agencies must consider only the alternatives the lead agency found reasonable. The statement of purpose and need would include the sponsor's goals. Sec. 1106 · Sec. 1102

Public tracker and portal for large reviews

CEQ would keep a public tracker with an entry for every project needing an EIS, and for any EA still open 13 months after its deadline. Entries show location, purpose and need, contacts for the sponsor and lead agency, schedule changes and litigation status. A common authorization portal, targeted for December 1, 2028, must include a way to tell local communities about notices of intent, public meetings and project status. Sec. 1132 · Sec. 1121

Record of the sponsor's outreach before applying

A sponsor would file, with its application, a record of stakeholder engagement of up to 20 pages. It lists pre-application contacts with state, local and tribal officials and community entities, the meeting dates, a brief summary of feedback and how feedback shaped the proposal. The bill requires the record, not any particular outreach. Sec. 1109 · Sec. 1102

Corridor work and routine drilling placed outside NEPA

Grid capacity work in or next to an existing right-of-way, and interstate gas pipeline repair, looping and compression in covered areas, would be outside NEPA and would not be a section 106 undertaking. Routine oil, gas and geothermal drilling activities on public and national forest land would not be a major Federal action and would carry no NEPA requirements. Clean Water Act and Clean Air Act duties remain for pipelines. Sec. 2102 · Sec. 2229

Public comment on federal water quality criteria

EPA would have to use rulemaking under 5 U.S.C. 553 when it develops any section 304(a) criterion. Final criteria, and a definition of waters of the United States, would be added to the actions reviewed first in the courts of appeals under section 509(b)(1). Sec. 1201

Environmental review of tribal rights-of-way

A tribe could grant rights-of-way over its own land without Interior's approval if it follows a tribal regulation Interior has approved. The regulation must provide an environmental review that informs the public, gives a reasonable opportunity to comment and requires the tribe to answer each relevant and substantive comment before approval. Interior's approval of a tribal regulation is not subject to NEPA, section 106 or the Endangered Species Act. Sec. 2212

State, tribal and local governments as cooperating agencies

The designation test names jurisdiction by law over an environmental effect. It does not mention special expertise. The lead agency must identify and invite likely agencies within 30 days of the notice of intent or EA decision, and each has 7 days to accept or decline. A cooperating agency's comments are limited to matters within its jurisdiction by law, and designation gives it no authority over the action. Sec. 1106

Who is consulted under section 106

The new consultation steps name state and tribal historic preservation officers, Indian tribes, Native Hawaiian organizations, local governments and the applicant. They do not name other consulting parties or the public. The agency 'may' consult the named parties on the area of effects and on historic properties, and must seek and, as applicable, incorporate their information if received within 30 days of a request. The agency's duty is limited to the area of potential effects it identifies. Sec. 2301

What counts as an effect or an undertaking under section 106

An adverse effect must be reasonably foreseeable and directly alter a property's characteristics. Visual, atmospheric or audible elements count only if they directly and significantly diminish the property's eligible characteristics. An undertaking would exclude work where the only federal involvement is a loan, loan guarantee or grant not made for historic preservation, and the area of potential effects would exclude areas affected only by a non-federal part of a project. Sec. 2301

Deadlines in section 106 consultation

The agency has 30 days to decide whether a proposal is an undertaking and 60 days after that to send a report when there are adverse effects. It then has 90 days to find the effects acceptable or reach an agreement, followed by 60 days of consultation, 45 days for formal Advisory Council comments and 30 days for a final determination. Consultation must end by the date the environmental document is due, unless the Secretary approves an extension for extraordinary circumstances. Sec. 2301

Silence and objections in section 106 consultation

If a preservation officer, tribe, Native Hawaiian organization or local government does not respond within 30 days of a request to review a report or finding, the agency proceeds. If a party raises substantive objections within 30 days, the agency has 30 days to explain how it considered them, and its duty to consult on that report is then fulfilled. The agency may decide, in its sole discretion, that other considerations outweigh historic preservation. Sec. 2301

Funding and oversight of state and tribal preservation offices

The bill authorizes $200,000,000 a year for the Fund for fiscal years 2027 to 2036 and makes deposits available from fiscal year 2028 without further appropriation. It adds $20,000,000 a year for fiscal years 2027 to 2030 in competitive grants for tribal preservation officers' online databases. Programs would be evaluated at least every 2 years, and a program that does not regularly meet consultation timelines is not consistent, which can lead to suspended grants and the Secretary taking over the officer's functions. Sec. 2302 · Sec. 2301

Time for Endangered Species Act section 7 consultation

The 90-day period becomes 60 days and the 150-day mark becomes 100 days. An applicant becomes eligible for relief under 5 U.S.C. 706(1) if an extended consultation passes 145 days without the required statement or consent. When an environmental document is required, consultation must finish by the date that document is due, unless the Secretary approves an extension for extraordinary circumstances. Sec. 1302

State takeover of Endangered Species Act consultation

Interior could assign its consultation duties to a state by written agreement of up to 10 years, for projects within that state. The state must publish its complete application at least 60 days before filing, take public comment for at least 30 days and respond to comments. Audits for the first 4 years go out for public comment and Interior must respond within 60 days. District courts would hear suits against a state that fails to carry out its duties, under the same standards as against Interior. A permit applicant may still choose to consult Interior. Sec. 1304

What a state can deny or condition under Clean Water Act section 401

Scope would be limited to 'water quality requirements', meaning sections 301, 302, 303, 306 and 307 and the federal, state and tribal laws implementing them. A certifying authority could deny only on clear and convincing evidence that no modification or reasonable condition would allow certification. Each condition needs clear and convincing evidence that it is necessary, the least burdensome and consistent with the federal permit. Sec. 1202

Time for a state to act on a section 401 certification

The federal agency sets a reasonable period of up to 1 year that must account for time needed under state or tribal law for public notice and comment. An extension needs the applicant's written request and the state's written concurrence. Certifying authorities must keep procedures for public notice of all applications and, as they see fit, hearings. For interstate gas pipelines and interstate transmission lines the period is up to 9 months and certification covers only direct point-source discharges. Sec. 1202

Reissuing nationwide Clean Water Act permits

Reissuing a nationwide permit would need no state consultation under ESA section 6(a), no federal consultation under ESA section 7(a)(2) and no section 106 consultation, and NEPA would be met by a programmatic assessment, if the permit does not narrow or raise the threshold for preconstruction requirements. The Corps would consider only effects on water quality requirements and treat a discharge into less than 2 acres as minimal. General permits would last 10 years. Sec. 1204 · Sec. 1203

Tribal consent to rights-of-way across tribal land

The bill strikes the references to the 1934 and 1936 Acts from that sentence, which as amended reads as covering lands of any tribe. A tribe could also grant rights-of-way itself under an approved regulation and set compensation and terms by negotiation or regulation. An approved regulation does not stop a tribe from consenting to a grant by Interior. Sec. 2212

FERC siting of a line over a state's objection

The corridor requirement is dropped. FERC 'shall' issue a permit for lines of 230 kilovolts or more that meet the findings, including one of the state-related triggers. The state's clock would start when the application is filed with the state. Developers of lines of 345 kilovolts or more could file at FERC at the same time as at the state, but FERC may not issue a permit until it makes one of the state-related findings. Sec. 2101

Views before FERC in a siting case

FERC must hold a public comment period of at least 45 days and must take landowner input into account. Affected landowners include owners of land crossed, used as a facility site or abutting the right-of-way, owners of a residence within 500 feet of the right-of-way, and federal, state and local governments. A state may file any part of its state record, and FERC must consider it. Sec. 2101

Eminent domain and compensation for a FERC-permitted line

Federal court procedure would follow Rule 71.1 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. Section 2101(e) also strikes subsection (f) of section 216 and replaces it with a cost allocation subsection. As written, the statutory fair market value language would no longer appear in section 216. The text does not change the Fifth Amendment's just-compensation requirement. Sec. 2101

Payments to places a transmission line crosses

The list would add all prudently incurred costs of payments to jurisdictions impacted by lines developed under section 216 or 225. The text does not require any payment or set an amount. Sec. 2101

States referring a utility's local transmission planning to FERC

Within 1 year FERC must set procedures for states to refer a public utility for investigation of its local transmission planning. FERC must notice the investigation for public comment and certify a team that includes up to 3 state-appointed members, then notice the team's report for comment. Remedies include changed processes, a lower return on equity, loss of the prudence presumption and a fine of up to $5,000,000 per facility paid to the referring state. Sec. 2104

Selected regional projects and FERC siting findings

A project selected in a regional or joint interregional plan would be considered to satisfy the findings in paragraphs (2) through (5) and, if applicable, (6) of section 216(b). The planning section lists required process elements, and none addresses state or public participation. Approval of a planning process is not a major Federal action under NEPA. Sec. 2103

Time to sue over a NEPA decision

A claim that an environmental review was not properly conducted under NEPA would be barred unless filed within 150 days after the authorization is published in the Federal Register (where an EIS was prepared) or made public (any other case), unless a shorter deadline applies. A supplemental document required by court order starts a new 150-day clock, and the claim is limited to information not in the earlier document. Sec. 1110

Who can bring a NEPA claim

Where an opportunity for comment was provided, the plaintiff must have submitted a substantive comment, detailed enough to put the agency on notice of the issue, during a public comment period. The plaintiff, or one of its members, must allege an actual or imminent injury in fact amounting to a direct harm. The section says it does not create a cause of action. Sec. 1110

Which court hears a NEPA claim

The court of appeals for the circuit where the project will be built or operated would have original and exclusive jurisdiction, with supplemental jurisdiction over related claims on the authorization's validity. Judges are to be randomly assigned to the maximum extent practicable. Courts must give substantial deference to the agency on the scope and contents of the document and must rule 'as expeditiously as possible', with no fixed deadline. Sec. 1110

Remedy when a court finds a NEPA violation

The only remedy would be a remand without vacatur or injunction, with instructions and a deadline of no more than 180 days. The authorization stays in effect and the project may proceed during the remand. A preliminary injunction is allowed only if the authorization is likely to violate a federal law other than NEPA and the National Historic Preservation Act, and the usual harm, equity and public interest tests are met. Sec. 1110

NEPA challenges to actions on tribal trust land

For an action authorizing use of land, minerals or other resources held in trust for a tribe, there would be no administrative or judicial review based on a failure to comply with NEPA. The exceptions are a claim by the tribe whose trust resources are used and a claim about reasonably foreseeable effects outside those resources. For tribe-initiated actions, the lead agency must presume the no-action alternative has negative effects. Sec. 1110 · Sec. 1106

Claims over gaps in the sponsor's outreach record

There would be no claim or judicial review of whether a final agency action complies with NEPA for any omission from the list described in section 3(22)(B). In the bill's definitions that list is the sponsor's pre-application engagement with state, local and tribal officials and community entities. Sec. 1110 · Sec. 1102

Time to sue over energy and mineral permits under any federal law

A civil action under federal law seeking review of a final agency action granting or denying an authorization for an energy, carbon capture or mineral project would be barred unless filed within 150 days after the action is made public, which must happen within 3 days of the action. Courts must expedite, and a remand deadline is up to 180 days unless a longer time is needed to comply with law. Sec. 2201

Challenges to Clean Water Act section 404 permits

Suits over individual and general permits, verifications under a general permit, and EPA approval of a state permit program would be barred unless filed within 150 days. Where a draft was published in the Federal Register with at least 30 days of comment, the plaintiff must have filed a detailed comment and the claim must relate to it. A court must remand and may not vacate or enjoin a permit unless activities would present an imminent and substantial danger to human health or the environment for which no other equitable remedy is available. Sec. 1205

Time and court for challenges to biological opinions

Any person could seek review of a biological opinion only in the D.C. Circuit, by a petition filed within 150 days after the opinion is made public on the agency's website. Sec. 1305

Time, comment and court rules for section 106 claims

A claim would be barred unless filed within 150 days after the later of the final agency action being made public and the preservation officer being notified. The plaintiff must have suffered or imminently will suffer direct harm and, where there was a comment period, filed a substantive and unique comment by the noticed deadline on the same subject. Claims go to the courts of appeals, with substantial deference to the agency. Sec. 2301

Governments that raise issues in consultation keep their right to sue

The direct-harm and comment requirements would not apply where a state or tribal preservation officer, tribe, Native Hawaiian organization or local government gave detailed written notice during consultation that identified the issues. The exemption does not name other consulting parties or the public. Sec. 2301

Remedy when a court finds a section 106 violation

The only remedy would be a remand without vacatur or injunction, with a deadline of no more than 180 days. A preliminary injunction is allowed only if the action likely violates a federal law other than the historic preservation division of title 54 or NEPA, and the usual tests are met. Sec. 2301

Challenges to section 106 programmatic agreements

Only signatories could sue over how a programmatic agreement is implemented; no court would have jurisdiction over a suit by anyone else. Federal agencies must develop programmatic agreements for routine and repetitive categories, and a state or tribal officer's agreement for the same category would apply. Sec. 2301

Court review of state certification decisions

A court reviewing a denial must put the burden on the certifying authority to show by clear and convincing evidence that no reasonable modification or condition would allow certification. For a challenged condition, the authority must show by clear and convincing evidence that it is necessary, least burdensome and consistent with the federal permit. A waiver of certification would not be subject to judicial review. Sec. 1202

Asking an agency to revoke or change an issued permit

For projects with non-federal sponsors, a federal agency could not take new action to revoke, suspend, amend or alter an authorization in effect on or after September 16, 2026, except in five cases. These include a court order, a material breach or violation of law, fraud, a need to prevent specific, urgent, substantial and proximate harm based on new information, or a request from the holder. The agency carries the burden by clear and convincing evidence. Sec. 1401

Objecting to a tribe's handling of a right-of-way

An interested party that has exhausted tribal remedies could petition Interior to review a tribe's compliance with its approved regulation. If Interior finds a violation it may act, including rescinding approval of the regulation, after the tribe gets a formal hearing and a chance to cure. Sec. 2212

Courts for challenges to transmission authorizations

Any order of a federal agency, or of a state agency acting under federal law, to issue, condition or deny a federal authorization for a transmission facility would be reviewed in the courts that review FERC orders under Federal Power Act section 313. 'Federal authorization' would include a Clean Water Act section 401 certification and other state approvals required under federal law, except under the Coastal Zone Management Act. Sec. 2101

Where do supporters and critics disagree?

Can a later administration undo an unlawful permit?

Bill section 1401: “the Federal agency is required to take such action by order of a court of competent jurisdiction”
