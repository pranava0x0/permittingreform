## Summary

Moves NEPA's definitions from section 111 to a new section 3 and expands them. ([p. 5, line 22 to p. 5, line 24](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=5)) New terms include agency chief environmental review and permitting officers, eNEPA technologies, key permitting agencies, a 20-page record of stakeholder engagement, record of decision, and statement of purpose and need. ([p. 6, line 1 to p. 6, line 3](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=6))

The bill lists 14 kinds of action that are not major federal actions and so fall outside NEPA. ([p. 16, line 13 to p. 16, line 14](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=16)) A project whose only federal involvement is a loan or loan guarantee is out. ([p. 17, line 1 to p. 17, line 3](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=17)) So are routine administrative actions under $10 million, and repair or replacement of lifeline infrastructure that adds no more than 20 percent capacity. ([p. 18, line 18 to p. 18, line 20](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=18))

The list also excludes geothermal observation test projects, transmission upgrades under new Federal Power Act section 216A(b), covered Natural Gas Act pipeline work, actions another agency already evaluated, and Army Corps continuing-authority or environmental-infrastructure projects with a federal share of $50 million or less. ([p. 19, line 20 to p. 19, line 22](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=19))

## Key points

- Loans and loan guarantees alone no longer make a project a major federal action ([p. 17, line 1 to p. 17, line 3](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=17))
- Lifeline infrastructure repair and replacement excluded if capacity grows by 20 percent or less and no new through lanes ([p. 19, line 8 to p. 19, line 10](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=19))
- Corps continuing-authority and environmental infrastructure projects excluded up to a $50 million federal share ([p. 21, line 1 to p. 21, line 3](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=21))
- New 'key permitting agency' list: USDA, Commerce, Interior, Energy, Transportation, Defense, EPA, FERC, NRC, DHS, HUD, the Corps, ACHP ([p. 15, line 7 to p. 15, line 9](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=15))
- Defines a 20-page record of stakeholder engagement documenting pre-application outreach ([p. 21, line 23 to p. 21, line 25](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=21))

### Current law

NEPA section 111 (added by the Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023) lists seven exclusions: non-federal actions with no or minimal federal funding or involvement, general revenue sharing, loans and loan guarantees where the agency lacks sufficient control over their use, SBA loan guarantees, enforcement actions, extraterritorial actions, and non-discretionary actions.

The bill makes a loan or loan guarantee an exclusion on its own terms when it is the only federal involvement. It covers grants and cost-share awards where the agency lacks predominant control. It adds seven exclusions: routine administrative actions up to $10 million, lifeline infrastructure repair, geothermal test projects, in-corridor transmission work, in-corridor gas pipeline work, actions another agency already reviewed, and Corps projects with a federal share up to $50 million.

### EPRA 2024 (reported S. 4753)

EPRA 2024 did not amend NEPA definitions.

### SPEED Act (House-passed H.R. 4776)

The SPEED Act (sec. 2(f)) added grants and cost-share awards to the financial-assistance exclusion, required 'complete' agency control before funding counts, excluded Farm Service Agency loans and actions already reviewed by another agency, and said federal funding alone cannot make an action major. It also defined 'reasonably foreseeable' effects as those with a close causal relationship to the project. The Senate bill has no such definition in NEPA and no limit on the scope of effects an agency may study; its list of excluded actions is longer.
