## Summary

Adds a judicial review section to NEPA. ([p. 67, line 13 to p. 67, line 13](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=67)) Suits challenging an authorization on NEPA grounds must be filed within 150 days. ([p. 68, line 1 to p. 68, line 2](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=68)) The party must allege an actual or imminent injury amounting to direct harm. ([p. 68, line 18 to p. 68, line 20](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=68)) Where a comment period was offered, the party must also have filed a sufficiently detailed comment. ([p. 68, line 11 to p. 68, line 14](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=68)) The federal court of appeals for the circuit where the project sits gets original and exclusive jurisdiction, with random judge assignment. ([p. 68, line 23 to p. 69, line 2](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=68))

If a court finds a NEPA violation, the only remedy is remand without vacatur or injunction, with a fix deadline of no more than 180 days. ([p. 69, line 24 to p. 70, line 3](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=69)) The permit stays in effect and the project can proceed during the remand. ([p. 71, line 15 to p. 71, line 18](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=71)) A preliminary injunction is available only for a likely violation of a law other than NEPA or the National Historic Preservation Act. ([p. 70, line 17 to p. 70, line 23](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=70))

Courts must give substantial deference to agencies on the scope and content of environmental documents. ([p. 73, line 25 to p. 74, line 2](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=73)) No NEPA suit may be brought over an action on tribal trust lands unless the tribe itself sues or effects reach beyond trust lands. ([p. 73, line 8 to p. 73, line 11](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=73)) No suit may rest on an omission from the list described in section 3(22)(B) of NEPA as amended. ([p. 74, line 7 to p. 74, line 8](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=74))

## Key points

- 150-day statute of limitations ([p. 68, line 1 to p. 68, line 2](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=68))
- Only a party with a direct-harm injury can sue, and where a comment period was offered it must have commented ([p. 68, line 10 to p. 68, line 12](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=68))
- Court of appeals has original and exclusive jurisdiction ([p. 69, line 1 to p. 69, line 2](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=69))
- Remand without vacatur is the sole NEPA remedy; the project continues during the remand ([p. 69, line 24 to p. 70, line 1](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=69))
- No preliminary injunction for NEPA or NHPA violations alone ([p. 70, line 17 to p. 70, line 22](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=70))
- Substantial deference to agencies on the scope and contents of the document ([p. 73, line 25 to p. 74, line 2](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=73))

## Comparison notes

- [SPEED Act (H.R. 4776), engrossed in House](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr4776eh/html/BILLS-119hr4776eh.htm)
- [Energy Permitting Reform Act of 2024 (S. 4753), reported in Senate](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-118s4753rs/html/BILLS-118s4753rs.htm)

### Current law

NEPA has no limitations period of its own, so the general 6-year limit on suits against the United States applies, with 2 years for FAST-41 covered projects and 150 days for highway and transit projects. There is no statutory prior-comment rule, cases start in district court, and courts can vacate or enjoin an approval.

### EPRA 2024 (reported S. 4753)

EPRA 2024 (sec. 101) set a 150-day limit for energy and mineral project authorizations and a 180-day remand deadline. It did not require prior comment, did not change standing, and left courts their ordinary remedies. That section survives as section 2201 of this bill.

### SPEED Act (House-passed H.R. 4776)

The SPEED Act (sec. 3) had the same core: 150 days, prior comment, direct harm, substantial deference, remand without vacatur or injunction, a 180-day fix deadline, and the tribal trust carve-out. Differences: the House bill barred challenges to the creation of categorical exclusions, required courts to rule within 180 days of receiving the record and appeals within another 180, and applied its remand rule to court orders entered before enactment. The Senate bill drops those three, moves cases to the courts of appeals with random assignment, lets construction continue during a remand in express terms, and allows a preliminary injunction when a different federal law is likely violated.

[p. 72, line 3](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=72) (indent level 3)

“(A) IN GENERAL.—For purposes of a claim—

[p. 72, line 5](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=72) (indent level 4)

“(i) a supplemental or revised environmental document for an authorization, when required by court order, shall be considered to be a separate environmental document; and

“(ii) the deadline for filing a claim for judicial review of an authorization described in clause (i) shall be the date that is 150 days after the date on which the authorization for which the supplemental environmental document is prepared is made public by the agency, unless a shorter time is specified in the Federal law pursuant to which judicial review is authorized.

“(8) ACTIONS FOR USE OF TRIBAL TRUST RESOURCES.—

[p. 73, line 3](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=73) (indent level 3)

“(A) IN GENERAL.—Except as provided in subparagraph (B), for any action that authorizes the use of lands, minerals, or other resources held in trust at the time of a final agency action by the United States for the benefit of an Indian Tribe, there shall be no administrative or judicial review of such final agency action based on a claim of failure to comply with the requirements of this Act.

[p. 73, line 12](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=73) (indent level 3)

“(B) EXCEPTIONS.—Subparagraph (A) shall not apply to an action for administrative or judicial review—

[p. 73, line 15](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=73) (indent level 4)

“(i) brought by the Indian Tribe for which the United States holds the lands, minerals, or other resources in trust; or

[p. 73, line 18](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=73) (indent level 4)

“(ii) that involves reasonably foreseeable effects of the final agency action that occur outside the lands, minerals, or other resources held in trust by the United States for the benefit of an Indian Tribe.

[p. 73, line 23](https://pranava0x0.github.io/permittingreform/bill.pdf#page=73) (indent level 1)

“(d) ROLE OF COURT.—In reviewing a claim of whether an authorization complies with the requirements of this Act, a court shall afford substantial deference to the agency relating to the scope and contents of an environmental document.

“(e) PROHIBITION ON CERTAIN CLAIMS AND JUDICIAL REVIEW.—Notwithstanding any other provision of law, there shall not be a claim or judicial review of whether a final agency action complies with the requirements of this Act for any omission from the list described in section 3(22)(B).
