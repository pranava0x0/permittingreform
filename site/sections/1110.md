## Summary

Adds a judicial review section to NEPA. Suits challenging an authorization on NEPA grounds must be filed within 150 days. The party must allege an actual or imminent injury amounting to direct harm. Where a comment period was offered, the party must also have filed a sufficiently detailed comment. The federal court of appeals for the circuit where the project sits gets original and exclusive jurisdiction, with random judge assignment.

If a court finds a NEPA violation, the only remedy is remand without vacatur or injunction, with a fix deadline of no more than 180 days. The permit stays in effect and the project can proceed during the remand. A preliminary injunction is available only for a likely violation of a law other than NEPA or the National Historic Preservation Act.

Courts must give substantial deference to agencies on the scope and content of environmental documents. No NEPA suit may be brought over an action on tribal trust lands unless the tribe itself sues or effects reach beyond trust lands. No suit may rest on an omission from the sponsor's stakeholder engagement record.

## Comparison notes

- [SPEED Act (H.R. 4776), engrossed in House](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr4776eh/html/BILLS-119hr4776eh.htm)
- [Energy Permitting Reform Act of 2024 (S. 4753), reported in Senate](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-118s4753rs/html/BILLS-118s4753rs.htm)

### Current law

NEPA has no limitations period of its own, so the general 6-year limit on suits against the United States applies, with 2 years for FAST-41 covered projects and 150 days for highway and transit projects. There is no statutory prior-comment rule, cases start in district court, and courts can vacate or enjoin an approval.

### EPRA 2024 (reported S. 4753)

EPRA 2024 (sec. 101) set a 150-day limit for energy and mineral project authorizations and a 180-day remand deadline. It did not require prior comment, did not change standing, and left courts their ordinary remedies. That section survives as section 2201 of this bill.

### SPEED Act (House-passed H.R. 4776)

The SPEED Act (sec. 3) had the same core: 150 days, prior comment, direct harm, substantial deference, remand without vacatur or injunction, a 180-day fix deadline, and the tribal trust carve-out. Differences: the House bill barred challenges to the creation of categorical exclusions, required courts to rule within 180 days of receiving the record and appeals within another 180, and applied its remand rule to court orders entered before enactment. The Senate bill drops those three, moves cases to the courts of appeals with random assignment, lets construction continue during a remand in express terms, and allows a preliminary injunction when a different federal law is likely violated.
