Sec. 1101. Purposes; procedure; savings provision

Rewrites NEPA's opening section. Adds a statement that NEPA prescribes a procedure for considering and informing the public about the reasonably foreseeable environmental effects of major federal actions, producing an informational document, and a savings clause that NEPA mandates no particular outcome.

Sec. 1102. Definitions

Moves NEPA's definitions from section 111 to a new section 3 and expands them. New terms include agency chief environmental review and permitting officers, eNEPA technologies, key permitting agencies, a 20-page record of stakeholder engagement, record of decision, and statement of purpose and need. The bill lists 14 kinds of action that are not major federal actions and so fall outside NEPA. A project whose only federal involvement is a loan or loan guarantee is out. So are routine administrative actions under $10 million, and repair or replacement of lifeline infrastructure that adds no more than 20 percent capacity. The list also excludes geothermal observation test projects, transmission upgrades under new Federal Power Act section 216A(b), covered Natural Gas Act pipeline work, actions another agency already evaluated, and Army Corps continuing-authority or environmental-infrastructure projects with a federal share of $50 million or less.

Sec. 1105. Procedure for determination of level of review

Expands when an agency can skip an environmental document. An agency may apply one or a combination of categorical exclusions (but may not stack the same size-based exclusion repeatedly), may rely on a categorical exclusion Congress created, and may treat a state or tribal environmental review, or compliance with another federal law, as serving NEPA's function. A project sponsor can ask the lead agency to stop an environmental assessment and go straight to an environmental impact statement. If the lead agency decides no environmental document is needed, no other agency may prepare one.

Sec. 1106. Timely and unified Federal reviews

Rewrites NEPA section 107, the core of the review process. Environmental assessments are due in 1 year and environmental impact statements in 2 years, measured from the earlier of the agency's determination or its notice that the application is complete. A record of decision follows a final EIS within 45 days. Agencies in the review must decide all authorizations within 90 days, and agencies outside it within 120 days. Lead agency designation gets a clock, with the CEQ Chairman resolving disputes within 21 days by a final, unreviewable decision. The Chairman can also order a resolution of interagency disputes, and that order counts as full compliance with any interagency consultation obligation under federal law. The lead agency must drop any alternative that does not meet the purpose and need or the goals of a non-federal sponsor. Agencies need not consider science published after the notice of intent. Public scoping comment periods run 45 to 60 days, or 75 with the sponsor's consent. If an agency defies a Chairman's dispute order and a deadline is missed, then after 30 days its permitting officers and senior executives must spend at least half their workweek on the overdue review. Once any deadline is 60 days overdue, the agency head may not travel more than 25 miles from the duty station until the document or permit issues. The Secretary of Defense is exempt. Project sponsors can sue over missed deadlines. The court must rule within 120 days and can order a decision within 60 days.

Sec. 1109. Applications for authorizations

Sets an application clock: within 60 days an agency must say whether an application is complete, and within another 60 days it must tell the sponsor whether the action is excluded from NEPA, needs an environmental assessment, or gets a notice of intent for an EIS. Applicants must submit a list of all known permits, a record of stakeholder engagement, and a statement of purpose and need.

Sec. 1110. Judicial review

Adds a judicial review section to NEPA. Suits challenging an authorization on NEPA grounds must be filed within 150 days. The party must allege an actual or imminent injury amounting to direct harm. Where a comment period was offered, the party must also have filed a sufficiently detailed comment. The federal court of appeals for the circuit where the project sits gets original and exclusive jurisdiction, with random judge assignment. If a court finds a NEPA violation, the only remedy is remand without vacatur or injunction, with a fix deadline of no more than 180 days. The permit stays in effect and the project can proceed during the remand. A preliminary injunction is available only for a likely violation of a law other than NEPA or the National Historic Preservation Act. Courts must give substantial deference to agencies on the scope and content of environmental documents. No NEPA suit may be brought over an action on tribal trust lands unless the tribe itself sues or effects reach beyond trust lands. No suit may rest on an omission from the list described in section 3(22)(B) of NEPA as amended.

Sec. 1121. Council on Environmental Quality

Replaces NEPA section 209 with an eNEPA technology mandate. Within 60 days CEQ must publish data standards for permitting data, within 120 days guidance on minimum functional requirements (automated project screening, case management, GIS tools, AI-assisted comment analysis, administrative-record tools), and agencies must begin implementing within 180 days. CEQ and the key permitting agencies build an ePermit unified interagency data system and a public authorization portal hosted by GSA. A pilot is due within a year and the system by December 1, 2028, 'to the maximum extent practicable'. Congress gets access to the data, fine-tuning procedures and prompt configurations agencies create or adapt for AI tools, but not proprietary or general pre-training materials. CEQ also sets outcome-based performance measures for agencies and staff. Authorizes $5 million a year for NEPA.

Sec. 1201. Water quality criteria

Sec. 1202. Clean Water Act permitting and State certification

Rewrites Clean Water Act section 401 state certification. Certification is limited to water quality requirements. A state may deny only on clear and convincing evidence that no condition could make the project comply, and may impose only conditions that are necessary, least burdensome, and consistent with the federal permit. The process gets a prefiling meeting, a defined complete application, a 3-day receipt acknowledgment, a 90-day completeness notice, and a review period of up to 1 year that can be extended only if the applicant asks in writing and the certifying authority agrees in writing. Silence is a waiver. Denials must name the specific water quality requirement violated. On judicial review the burden of proof falls on the state by clear and convincing evidence. For interstate gas pipelines and bulk-power transmission lines, certification covers only direct point source discharges and must be decided within 9 months.

Sec. 1204. Permits for dredged or fill material

Sec. 1401. Maintaining Federal authorizations or permits for projects with non-Federal sponsors

For projects with non-federal sponsors, a federal agency may not revoke, suspend, amend, or otherwise interfere with a federal authorization in effect on or after September 16, 2026. There are five exceptions. A court can order the action. The holder can have materially breached the permit or the law, or obtained the permit by fraud. The action can be needed to prevent specific, urgent, substantial and proximate harm to life, property, national security or defense, based on new information not considered in the original review. Or the holder can ask for it. Agencies must give written notice with the statutory authority and evidence. The sponsor can sue in district court, where the agency carries the burden by clear and convincing evidence and classified information is reviewed in camera. A winning sponsor gets the permit reinstated, an injunction against repeat interference without a court order, attorney fees, delay costs, and an additional 25 to 50 percent of all project costs incurred, paid from the Judgment Fund without appropriation. Where a stop-work order issued within the year before enactment was enjoined, the same judge keeps jurisdiction.

Sec. 1402. Ensuring fairness for specific types of covered projects

Creates a cause of action for applicants in 46 named energy project types, plus any similar type a court certifies. The list runs from onshore and offshore wind, solar and storage to coal, gas, oil, nuclear, geothermal, transmission, pipelines, mining, hydrogen, hydropower, LNG and carbon storage. The applicant must show a 'pattern of disparate treatment': an intentional, statistically demonstrated rise in denials or delays for that project type, compared with the prior 5 years. The pattern must last 180 days after enactment for permits without a NEPA document, or 280 days for those with one. Executive orders and official statements showing bias count as supporting evidence. The government must produce raw statistics for the year of the suit and the 5 years before it, or face an adverse inference. Permits for these projects that need no NEPA document must be decided within 1 year of a complete application. Remedies are an injunction, fees, economic damages, and 50 to 100 percent of the expected construction cost, paid from the Judgment Fund. The applicant must finish the project or repay. Class actions are barred, and suits cannot be filed until 180 or 280 days after enactment.

Sec. 1403. Avoiding unreasonable delays in permitting

Gives applicants for permits that need no environmental document a right to sue in district court when the 1-year deadline in section 1402(c) is missed. The agency is excused if it proves the delay was caused by a disaster, a national emergency, fraud, withheld information, or a lapse in appropriations of more than 14 days. Courts decide within 120 days and can order a decision within 60 days.

Sec. 2101. Transmission permitting

Overhauls FERC's backstop siting authority under Federal Power Act section 216. FERC must issue a construction or modification permit for an interstate line of 230 kilovolts or more, or an advanced-conductor line at a voltage FERC sets, when it makes the required findings. The line no longer has to sit in a national interest corridor designated by the Energy Department. The state's clock for deciding now starts on the date the application is filed with the state. FERC must find the line is in the public interest, protects or benefits consumers, and improves reliability. For lines of 345 kilovolts or more the developer may file at FERC at the same time as at the state. FERC must take landowner input, consider the state record, and give a 45-day comment period. Costs go only to customers who benefit, in line with cost causation, and customers with no or trivial benefit cannot be charged involuntarily. Clean Water Act section 401 certifications and other state approvals under federal law become 'Federal authorizations' coordinated by FERC as lead agency (Interior for offshore), with challenges heard in the courts of appeals. ERCOT is excluded.

Sec. 2102. Reconductoring and other activities in existing rights-of-way

Adds Federal Power Act section 216A: work that maintains or increases grid capacity inside an existing right-of-way (reconductoring, higher operating voltage, added circuits, advanced transmission technologies, added storage at an existing facility, a parallel circuit) is categorically excluded from NEPA, is not a major federal action, and is not an undertaking under the National Historic Preservation Act. It also amends Natural Gas Act section 7 so that repair, replacement, looping, compression and capacity-enhancement of interstate gas pipelines inside an existing right-of-way or previously reviewed footprint are not major federal actions or historic-preservation undertakings.

Sec. 2103. Transmission planning

Adds Federal Power Act sections 224 and 225. The Energy Department studies transmission constraints and congestion every 3 years and may identify constrained geographic areas. FERC must issue a rule within 180 days requiring every planning region to file a regional planning process and joint interregional processes with its neighbors within 2 years, updated every 4 years. The processes must use common assumptions, a fixed list of six benefits, and selection criteria of improved reliability, consumer benefit and public interest. Costs are allocated to beneficiaries roughly commensurate with benefits. Compliance with the FERC planning orders published on June 11, 2024, December 6, 2024 and April 28, 2025 (Order 1920 and its two rehearing orders) counts as compliance for regional planning. A project selected in a regional or interregional plan is deemed to meet most of the findings for a FERC backstop permit. Approving a planning process is not a major federal action under NEPA.

Sec. 2104. Improvements relating to local transmission planning and development

Adds Federal Power Act section 226. One or more states may refer a utility to FERC for an investigation of its local transmission planning. FERC forms an investigative team with up to 3 state appointees. If the team finds the utility's process is not selecting the more efficient or cost-effective local projects, or is crowding out regional planning, FERC may order process changes, cut the utility's return on equity on local projects, remove the presumption of prudence, or fine the utility. A fine, paid to the referring state, can reach $5 million for each local facility developed or energized from 60 days before the referral that FERC finds imprudent and harmful to that state's customers.

Sec. 2107. Ratepayer protection

Congress finds it is not just and reasonable to charge data center, AI and cryptocurrency loads less than the embedded and incremental cost of the transmission they need. FERC must revise its 1994 transmission pricing policy within 270 days, propose a rule 270 days after that, and finish within a further year. New Federal Power Act section 228 covers data center loads of 20 megawatts or more. None of the incremental cost of serving them (generation, transmission, distribution) may be recovered from other customers. Recovery continues even if the data center leaves, and utilities must collect financial assurance before building upgrades. States may charge these loads more than cost and credit the surplus to other ratepayers. They may run open seasons that award service by ratepayer benefit, whatever the queue order. They may require the load to bring new generation, require curtailable service, and treat data centers less favorably than other industrial customers. The section applies to loads interconnecting after enactment.

Sec. 2108. Dispatchable energy systems market access

Opens wholesale markets to aggregations of customer-sited batteries, fuel cells and generators that can respond within 15 minutes (5 kilowatts minimum per device, 100 kilowatts per aggregation). After 18 months, grid operator rules that block them are unjust and unreasonable. Grid operators file compliance tariffs within 180 days. Unless state law or the state regulator provides otherwise, distribution utilities in organized markets must interconnect these resources; for public power utilities and other section 201(f) entities the requirement is voluntary. They must also accept wholesale metering data. A device paid by a state retail program cannot be paid twice for the same service; FERC orders disgorgement and can impose penalties of up to 3 times the amount for each day it was knowingly kept. The Energy Department runs a voluntary streamlined local permitting program for rooftop solar, batteries and vehicle chargers, with $20 million a year authorized for fiscal years 2026 through 2031.

Sec. 2201. Accelerating claims

A 150-day statute of limitations for any lawsuit challenging a federal agency's grant or denial of an authorization for an energy, carbon dioxide, mineral or wildfire fuel-reduction project, unless the underlying law sets a shorter one. Covered authorizations include leases, drilling permits, rights-of-way, mining plans, Endangered Species Act statements and permits, and forest restoration approvals. Agencies must publish the decision within 3 days. Courts must expedite the case, and a remand cannot give the agency more than 180 days unless the law requires longer. Agencies must tell the project sponsor about any suit within 30 days.

Sec. 2213. Accelerating renewable energy permitting

Sec. 2301. Historic preservation

Rewrites section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act. An 'adverse effect' must be reasonably foreseeable and must directly alter a historic property. Temporary alterations are excluded. Visual, atmospheric and audible effects are excluded unless they directly and significantly diminish the property. An 'undertaking' excludes projects whose only federal tie is a loan, guarantee or grant, the non-federal parts of a project, minor repairs, permit renewals and post-approval operations. The process gets deadlines: 30 days to decide whether a project is an undertaking, 60 days for the agency's report, 90 days for a determination, then 60 days of consultation, 45 days for Advisory Council comments and 30 days for a final decision. Consultation must be finished by the time the NEPA document is completed, unless the Secretary grants an extension for extraordinary circumstances. State and tribal preservation officers who do not respond in 30 days are passed over, and the agency may decide in its sole discretion that other considerations outweigh preservation. Courts give substantial deference and may only remand without vacatur or injunction; a preliminary injunction is available only for a likely violation of another law. Claims go to the courts of appeals, within 150 days, from parties who commented and suffered direct harm. A suit over a missed consultation deadline goes to a court where the project is located. Non-signatories cannot challenge programmatic agreements. State and tribal programs are evaluated every 2 years and lose grants if they miss consultation timelines.

Sec. 2302. Funding for the Historic Preservation Fund; use and availability; grants for Tribal Historic Preservation officers; additional funding

Authorizes $200 million a year for deposit in the Historic Preservation Fund for fiscal years 2027 through 2036. Deposits become available the next fiscal year without a further appropriation. Creates a $20 million-a-year grant program (fiscal years 2027 through 2030) for tribal historic preservation officers to build online databases of records and of properties of traditional religious or cultural importance, with protections against disclosing sensitive site locations.
